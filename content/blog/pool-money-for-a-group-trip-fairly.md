Ask anyone who has organised a group trip what the hardest part was, and it is rarely the destination. It's the money: fronting deposits, keeping track of who has paid, and the awkward message to the friend who hasn't.

Pooling money works when everyone agrees the rules before anyone pays, and when everyone can see where the money is. Here is how to set it up.

## Agree four things before anyone pays

**1. The amount per person, and what it covers.** Write it down, for example: "KES 8,000 each covers the house, the boat trip and fuel. Food and drinks are on you." Most money disagreements come from people assuming different things were included.

**2. How the cost is split.** Equal shares are simplest and right most of the time. Split by use only when it's obvious and big, like a couple sharing a room versus singles, or someone who is only coming for one night. Don't itemise every soda.

**3. When it's due.** Tie it to the bookings: a deposit when the place is booked, the rest a week before you go. A date beats "when you can".

**4. What happens if someone drops out.** Decide now, while nobody is dropping out. Common rules: full refund until the booking is paid, then a refund only if they find a replacement; or deposits are non-refundable. Any rule works if it was agreed in advance.

## Keep the money in one visible place

The organiser's personal account is the worst place for group money. It mixes with their own, nobody else can see it, and it makes them the debt collector.

Use one place, separate from anyone's personal spending, where every member can see:

- how much has come in and from whom,
- how much has gone out and on what,
- what's left.

When everyone can see the pool, reminders stop being personal. "We're KES 6,000 short of the house deposit" lands differently from "Brian, you haven't paid".

## Pay bookings from the pool, not from a pocket

Once the money is in, pay for things from the pool. The organiser shouldn't front costs and claim them back later; that's how people end up quietly out of pocket. If a booking needs paying before everyone has contributed, agree who fronts it and record it as owed to them from the pool.

## Handle the awkward cases kindly

**Someone can't afford it.** Better to know early. Choose a cheaper plan, offer a payment schedule, or agree privately that someone covers part of their share. Don't make it public.

**Someone pays late.** Remind the whole group with the pool total, not the person by name. Most late payments are forgetfulness, not refusal.

**Someone drops out.** Apply the rule you agreed. If you didn't agree one, the fair default is: refund what hasn't been spent yet, and treat money already paid to a booking as spent unless a replacement takes their place.

**There's money left over.** Return it in equal shares, or roll it into the next plan if everyone agrees. Say which, so nobody wonders.

## Chamas already know this

Savings groups across Kenya, chamas, have run on these principles for generations: a clear contribution, a set date, rules agreed together and a record everyone can see. A trip pool is a small, short-lived chama with a fun ending. Borrow what works.

## The test of a fair pool

You've set it up well if, at any moment, anyone in the group can answer three questions without asking the organiser: how much have we collected, what has it paid for, and what's still owed. If they can, the money will never be what people remember about the trip.
