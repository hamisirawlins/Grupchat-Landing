Every group trip starts the same way. Someone posts "we should go somewhere", fifteen people react, and three weeks later the plan is buried under memes, voice notes and "I'm in, depending". The trip doesn't fail because people don't want to go. It fails because nobody can tell what has been decided.

Here is a way to get from "we should" to "we're booked" that works for six friends going to the coast, a family reunion upcountry, or a team outing.

## 1. Name one organiser, and give them a deputy

Groups don't plan; people do. Agree up front who is organising, and pick a second person who can answer questions when the organiser is busy. This isn't about control. It's so that "who's sorting the accommodation?" has an answer.

The organiser's job is to make decisions easy for everyone else, not to make every decision.

## 2. Decide three things before anything else

Most planning stalls because the group argues about details before agreeing on the shape. Settle these first, in this order:

1. **When.** Offer two or three date options and ask people to say which ones they can't do. "Can't" is easier to answer than "prefer".
2. **Roughly how much.** Give a range per person that includes travel, stay and activities. Say it out loud early; it's kinder than finding out at checkout that half the group can't afford the plan.
3. **What kind of trip.** Beach and rest, outdoors and active, or a city weekend. Not the exact place yet, just the kind.

Once those three are settled, choosing the actual place is quick, because most options have already ruled themselves out.

## 3. Put the plan somewhere that isn't the chat

A group chat is great for banter and terrible as a record. Decisions scroll away, and new joiners can't catch up. Keep the plan in one place everyone can open: the dates, the place, the cost per person, who's confirmed and what's still open.

It can be a shared note, a pinned message that the organiser keeps current, or an app built for it. What matters is that there is one answer to "what's the plan?" and it's always up to date.

## 4. Set a deadline that means something

"Let me know" has no end. "Confirm by Friday 6 pm so we can book" does. A deadline tied to a real consequence, like a booking that has to be paid, turns maybes into answers.

The most reliable way to confirm is money. A small deposit into a shared pool by the deadline separates the people who are coming from the people who would like to be.

## 5. Collect the money in one place, where everyone can see it

The organiser fronting the whole cost and chasing people for weeks afterwards is how friendships get tested. Instead:

- Agree the amount per person and the date it's due.
- Collect it into one place everyone can see, so nobody has to ask who has paid.
- Pay for bookings from that pool, so the organiser isn't out of pocket.
- Decide in advance what happens if someone drops out: refund, find a replacement, or lose the deposit.

We go deeper on this in [how to pool money for a group trip, fairly](/blog/pool-money-for-a-group-trip-fairly).

## A simple timeline

For a weekend trip, something like this works:

| When | What |
| --- | --- |
| 4–6 weeks before | Organiser named; dates, budget range and kind of trip agreed |
| 3–4 weeks before | Place chosen; cost per person set; deposit deadline shared |
| 2–3 weeks before | Deposits in; accommodation and transport booked from the pool |
| 1 week before | Final payments in; who's driving and what to bring settled |
| After | Photos shared; any money left over returned |

## The part that's easy to forget

Plan the getting-there and the getting-home as carefully as the destination. Most group trips go wrong on transport: who's driving, who's picking up whom, and what time the last people leave. Put it in the plan.

And when it's over, close the loop. Share the photos, settle any leftover money, and say who's organising next time. The groups that travel together often are the ones where planning never falls on the same person twice.
